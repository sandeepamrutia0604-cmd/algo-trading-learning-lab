    A stop-loss sells the whole position if the price falls to it; a take-profit sells if it rises to it. Set them on the order ticket when you buy, or press Exits on a position to change them. They are checked as the market advances, against each day's low and high. If a stock opens beyond a level it fills at the open instead (a gap), and a day that reaches both takes the stop. Slippage and charges apply as to any sale.

    Every backtest is only as good as the prices under it, and bad prices rarely look bad.     Return for the risk taken

    What do these numbers mean?
    Annual growth (CAGR)
    The yearly growth rate the result works out to, as if the profit had compounded evenly. Handy for comparing runs of different lengths.
    Volatility
    How much the account value jumps around from day to day, scaled to a year. Higher means a bumpier ride.
    Sharpe ratio
    Return earned for each unit of bumpiness. A common rule of thumb calls above 1 decent and above 2 excellent, but a short backtest can flatter it, so treat it as a clue, not a verdict.
    Sortino ratio
    Like Sharpe, but only the falling days count as risk. A strategy with big up-days and small down-days scores better here than on Sharpe.
    Calmar ratio
    Yearly growth divided by the worst fall: how much growth you got for the worst pain you sat through.
    Longest time under water
    The longest stretch the account stayed below a previous high, in trading days (about 21 make a month). It shows how long you might wait to be back in profit.
    Time in the market
    The share of days the strategy actually held shares. The rest of the time it sat in cash, so a low figure means the return was earned on little exposure.
    Profit factor
    All the money won on closed trades divided by all the money lost. Above 1 means it made money overall; many guides want 1.5 or more before trusting a strategy.
    Expectancy
    The average profit or loss per trade. It equals win rate × average win minus loss rate × average loss, so a strategy can win rarely and still have a positive expectancy.
    Payoff ratio
    Average win divided by average loss. A low win rate can still work if this is high, and a high win rate can still lose money if it is low.
    Longest losing streak
    The most losing trades in a row. A reminder of what you would have had to sit through.
    Beta
    How much the strategy has tended to move when the comparison moved 1%. 1 means in step, 0.5 means half as much, 0 means unrelated.
    Alpha
    The yearly return left over after taking out what the comparison's own moves (via beta) would explain. Positive means the strategy added something of its own.
    Correlation
    How closely the two moved together, from -1 (opposite) through 0 (unrelated) to 1 (in lockstep).

    Tries every combination of one or two of a strategy's settings on a training period, then runs the winner on a test period it has never seen. Picking the best of many settings on the same data you score them on is how overfitting happens; the test period is how you find out.
